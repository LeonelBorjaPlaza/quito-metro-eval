                                                     Abstract

            This paper studies whether the early air-quality effects of urban rail can be spatially concen-
        trated within a city. We examine Quito Metro Line 1, Ecuador’s first metro line, which opened in
        December 2023 and provides direct access to the city’s historic center, a dense destination where
        car access is difficult. The main analysis uses weekly peak-hour data from Quito’s monitoring
        network and estimates station-level effects with augmented synthetic control and conformal
        inference. A complementary citywide analysis uses satellite data for Latin American urban
        areas and synthetic difference-in-differences with placebo inference. In the preferred local
        specification, PM2.5 at the historic-center station, Centro, falls by 0.130 log points, equivalent to
        12.2 percent, in the first months of operation, before the late-2024 energy crisis. The estimate is
        nearly unchanged when later post-opening weeks are added but blackout and wildfire weeks are
        excluded. We show that Centro is the only station with a statistically significant negative PM2.5
        estimate in a spatial placebo exercise. The response is particulate-specific: local gas pollutants
        do not show comparable reductions. The citywide analysis finds that satellite aerosol optical
        depth (AOD) moves in the same particulate direction, while satellite gas outcomes do not show
        a consistent pattern. The findings show that, in this setting, the air-quality benefits of a metro
        are concentrated at a destination where car access is difficult, a pattern that simple corridor
        proximity cannot explain. The results suggest that large transit investments may deliver stronger
        environmental gains when complemented by policies that make car access less attractive in
        dense destinations.

1   Introduction
Cities invest heavily in urban rail partly because cleaner mass transit is expected to reduce congestion,
fuel combustion, and exposure to traffic-related pollution. Yet the environmental returns to these
investments are not automatic. A new metro can shift some travelers away from cars and road-based
public transport, but it can also free road space for other vehicles, affect some neighborhoods more
than others, and operate in cities where weather, topography, and non-transport shocks also shape
air quality. The relevant policy question is therefore not only whether urban rail improves air quality
on average, but where within the city these gains appear and what urban conditions make them more
likely.
    This paper studies that question in Quito, Ecuador, using the opening of the city’s first metro line
in December 2023. The setting is useful because Quito Metro Line 1 connects the city’s north-south
corridor to the historic center, a dense UNESCO World Heritage site where narrow streets, limited
parking, and high pedestrian activity make car access difficult. This geography creates a sharp
local test. If the metro reduces pollution mainly through proximity to the rail corridor, then nearby
monitoring stations should show similar changes. If instead the effect depends on destination access,
then the response should be strongest where the metro changes the cost of reaching a dense, car-
constrained destination. In Quito, Centro and Belisario provide this contrast. Centro, the monitoring
station inside the historic center, lies 0.61 kilometers from the nearest metro stop. Belisario, outside
the historic center, lies 0.77 kilometers from the nearest stop. The two stations are similarly close
to the metro, but only Centro captures access to the destination where the mechanism predicts the
clearest early effect.
    Existing evidence shows that urban rail and related transport policies can improve air quality.
Gendron-Carrier et al. (2022) estimate pollution effects around metro stations across cities and find
larger gains in more polluted settings. Other studies examine transit expansions, traffic restrictions,
or transport and emissions shocks at corridor or city scales (Chen and Whalley 2012; Li et al. 2019;
Goel and Gupta 2017; Lin et al. 2021; Chen et al. 2013). We build on this literature by asking
a more spatially specific question: when a large transit investment changes access to particular
destinations, do pollution gains concentrate in places where car access is difficult and rail becomes
a more attractive substitute? This distinction matters for both interpretation and policy. A null or
modest citywide average can still mask meaningful local gains, while a local gain near a destination
need not imply a smooth distance gradient around the entire corridor.
    We combine two empirical designs that match these two spatial scales. The main analysis uses


                                                   2
weekly peak-hour readings from Quito’s ground-based air-quality monitoring network and constructs
synthetic counterfactuals for exposed stations. Because the local donor pool contains only a small
number of monitoring stations, the preferred local estimator is augmented synthetic control with
station fixed effects, and inference relies on two-sided conformal p-values. We then place the local
evidence in broader context using satellite retrievals for Latin American urban areas. The citywide
analysis compares Quito to similar cities using synthetic difference-in-differences. The two designs
therefore answer different questions: the local design tests whether pollution changed at the predicted
destination, while the citywide design asks whether Quito experienced a detectable aggregate change
relative to a counterfactual constructed from comparable urban areas.
    The results show a localized particulate response at Quito’s historic center. In the preferred
local specification, PM2.5 at Centro falls significantly, by about 12 percent, during the first months
of operation, before Ecuador’s late-2024 blackout and wildfire disruptions. The estimate is nearly
unchanged when later post-opening weeks are added, as long as the blackout and wildfire weeks are
excluded. Belisario, the similarly close station outside the historic center, does not show comparable
evidence of a post-opening PM2.5 reduction. A leave-one-station-out spatial placebo exercise shows
that Centro is the only station with a statistically significant negative PM2.5 estimate during this
early period. The local pollutant pattern is also particulate-specific: NO2 is a well-fit null, CO
is uninformative, and SO2 turns positive only when weeks affected by the energy crisis enter the
estimation window.
    The citywide satellite evidence points in the same particulate direction, but we interpret it as
complementary rather than as the paper’s main causal test. In the preferred 100-city donor pool,
aerosol optical depth falls by 0.113 log points during the first months of operation, before Ecuador’s
late-2024 blackout and wildfire disruptions. The estimate is also negative across alternative donor
pools. Satellite gas outcomes do not show a consistent pattern. These results suggest that the local
particulate finding is not contradicted at the city scale, but they do not establish the mechanism.
Aerosol optical depth measures aerosol loading in the atmospheric column rather than surface PM2.5 ,
and the citywide comparison is more exposed to national shocks, regional atmospheric conditions,
and non-transport sources.
    The paper makes two contributions. First, it shows that the early air-quality benefits of a metro can
be concentrated at a destination where car access is difficult, rather than following simple proximity to
the rail corridor. This complements evidence on average pollution effects around transit stations and
at the city scale by showing why the location of effects within the city matters. The result also has a
direct policy implication: large transit investments may generate stronger environmental gains when
they are complemented by measures that make car use less attractive in dense destinations. Second,
the paper illustrates an empirical strategy for studying transport investments when exposure is local
but citywide shocks are important. A small-donor local synthetic-control design with conformal

inference provides the main evidence, while the citywide comparison asks whether Quito’s aggregate
pollution changed relative to similar Latin American cities after the metro opened. The findings
document where the particulate response appears and are consistent with destination-based mode
substitution, while leaving the direct traffic channel for future evidence on ridership, congestion,
and vehicle flows.

transport policy and air-quality evaluation.

2   Institutional Background
Quito is located in a narrow Andean valley on the eastern slopes of the Pichincha volcano, at
roughly 2,850 meters above sea level. Its topography matters for air quality. Steep terrain, frequent
temperature inversions, and stagnant-air episodes can limit dispersion and trap pollutants near the
surface (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2026; Cazorla 2016; Zalakeviciute et al. 2024). These conditions
make traffic-related emissions especially relevant for local exposure, particularly for fine particulate
matter. Prior air-quality research on Quito emphasizes that basin topography, diesel traffic, and
meteorological conditions have contributed to persistent particulate pollution above international
health guidelines (Cazorla 2016; Zalakeviciute et al. 2024).
    Quito’s historic center is central to the paper’s mechanism. The Centro Histórico is a UNESCO
World Heritage site, with dense activity, narrow colonial streets, limited parking, and high pedestrian
flows (Encyclopaedia Britannica 2026; UNESCO World Heritage Centre n.d.). These features make
automobile access costly even though the area is geographically central. A metro can therefore
affect pollution in the historic center by moving travelers along a corridor, and also by changing the
relative cost of reaching a dense destination where car access is difficult. This distinction motivates
the empirical design: Centro and Belisario are both close to Metro Line 1, but only Centro is located
in the historic center.
    Quito Metro Line 1 is the city’s first metro line. Commercial service began on December 1,
2023, after earlier trial operations were suspended. The line runs entirely underground for about
22.6 kilometers along Quito’s north-south valley corridor, connecting Quitumbe in the south with
El Labrador in the north through 15 stations (Transdev 2023). The end-to-end trip takes roughly 34
minutes, and the system was planned for several hundred thousand daily passengers once mature
(Oliver Wyman Forum 2024). One station, San Francisco, is located in the historic center, providing
direct metro access to the area monitored by the Centro station. Because the system opened only at
the end of 2023 and ridership was still ramping up during 2024, the estimates in this paper capture
early operational effects rather than long-run impacts.

    The first year of operation also coincided with an unusually disruptive national context. In 2024,
Ecuador experienced a severe drought that constrained hydroelectric generation and led to scheduled
electricity rationing beginning in September. The crisis intensified in October, and households and
firms responded by using diesel generators, a source of local combustion emissions unrelated to
the metro. Wildfires around Quito during the same period further degraded air quality (Vallejo et
al. 2025; Zalakeviciute et al. 2024). This institutional shock is important for the empirical design.
It motivates estimating a pre-disruption window that ends before the blackout and wildfire period,
a donut window that excludes the disruption weeks, and a full window that shows how estimates
change when those weeks are retained.

3       Data
We assemble two panels at two spatial scales. The first is a panel of ground-based air-quality monitors
in Quito. It measures surface pollution at fixed monitoring stations across the metropolitan area and
provides the main local evidence in the paper. The second is a panel of satellite retrievals across Latin
American urban areas. The city-level panel treats Quito’s transport system as a whole, recognizing
that a metro can affect surface traffic near stations and also indirectly across the broader urban
network. Given the broader Ecuadorian context of 2024, which combined a security emergency,
fiscal contraction, and a drought-driven energy crisis that may confound city-level comparisons,
we interpret the citywide analysis as complementary to the local station analysis rather than as the
paper’s primary estimate of exposure changes.
    We describe the treatment timing and the 2024 disruption that shapes the estimation windows
(Section 3.1), the air-quality monitoring station panel (Section 3.2), and the satellite-derived urban-
center panel (Section 3.3). We discuss the estimators in the empirical strategy section.

3.1      Treatment timing and the 2024 disruption

weekly level, with the post-opening indicator turning on in the ISO week1 that contains the opening
date, beginning November 27, 2023.

3.2      Local analysis: ground-based air monitoring data
The REMMAQ2 data consist of validated hourly station readings published by Quito’s municipal
environmental authority (REMMAQ 2026). They cover pollutant concentrations and meteorological
conditions across the metropolitan area. We use all eight stations with available data for the analysis
period: Belisario, Carapungo, Centro, Cotocollao, Guamaní, Los Chillos, San Antonio, and Tumbaco.
We study four pollutants, PM2.5 , CO, NO2 , and SO2 , and use temperature, humidity, wind speed,
wind direction, rainfall, solar radiation, and surface pressure as meteorological controls.
    Two stations are especially important for the design. Centro is located inside Quito’s historic
center, where narrow streets, limited parking, and high pedestrian activity make automobile access
unusually costly. Belisario is located near the Metro corridor but outside the historic center. Centro is
0.61 km from the nearest Line 1 stop, while Belisario is 0.77 km away. The two stations are therefore
similarly close to the metro but differ sharply in the urban setting most relevant to the mechanism.

4    Empirical Strategy
We estimate the effect of Quito’s first metro line on air pollution by constructing counterfactual
pollution paths for exposed locations after the start of commercial operations. Let Yit denote pollution
in unit i in week t, where the unit is a REMMAQ monitoring station in the local analysis or an urban
area in the citywide analysis. For each treated unit, the object of interest is the average post-opening
difference between observed pollution and the pollution path that would have occurred absent the
metro:

where τi is the treatment effect, T0 is the week of the metro opening, T1 is the number of post-
opening periods used in the estimate, and Ybit (0) is a synthetic counterfactual constructed from units
not assigned to treatment. The identifying assumption is that, after conditioning on pre-opening
pollution dynamics and weather, the synthetic comparison path approximates the untreated potential
outcome of the treated unit. This logic follows the synthetic-control tradition of constructing a
comparison path from weighted untreated units rather than relying on a single untreated comparison
unit (Abadie et al. 2010).
    The local station analysis is designed to distinguish two mechanisms. A corridor-proximity
mechanism predicts that pollution changes should be strongest at monitors close to Metro Line 1.
The destination-access mechanism predicts that effects should be more pronounced where the metro
changes the relative cost of reaching the historic center. This distinction is central in Quito because
Centro and Belisario are both close to Line 1, but only Centro lies inside the historic center, where
narrow colonial streets, limited parking, and high pedestrian activity make car access unusually
costly. We therefore first examine whether the data support a proximity interpretation. Belisario


                                                     10
provides the key discriminating comparison: if proximity to the line is sufficient to reduce pollution,
Belisario should also experience a decline; if the effect operates through mode substitution for trips
bound for the historic center, Belisario should not.
    We operationalize this test using the other REMMAQ stations as the local comparison group.
For each station, we construct a synthetic counterfactual from the remaining stations and examine
whether the post-opening deviation is unusual. This leave-one-out exercise serves two purposes.
First, it tests whether effects trace a general proximity pattern across the monitoring network. Second,
it verifies whether Belisario behaves as an untreated comparison station before it is used as part
of the donor pool for Centro. Thus, the role of Belisario is not circular: its null response is first a
diagnostic against the corridor-proximity hypothesis and then a validation of its inclusion as a donor
for the Centro counterfactual.
    We implement two related counterfactual estimators. The first is augmented synthetic control,
implemented with the augsynth package (Ben-Michael et al. 2021a,b). The second is synthetic
difference-in-differences, implemented with the synthdid package (Arkhangelsky et al. 2021, 2024).
Both estimators construct a counterfactual pollution path using untreated comparison units, but they
differ in how they use pre-treatment information, how they incorporate covariates, and in the type of
inference that is most credible in our two empirical settings.8

units. Standard synthetic control chooses weights w   b to make X0 w b close to X1 . Augmented synthetic
control adjusts the post-treatment synthetic-control contrast by an estimate of the remaining bias:

     The two estimators play different roles because the comparison sets differ sharply across analyses.
In the local REMMAQ analysis, the donor pool contains only the other Quito monitoring stations.
With such a small donor pool, the inference procedures recommended for SDID in larger panels are
not reliable as the main inferential basis: placebo and large-sample approximations have very little
information when there are only a few untreated units. Accordingly, local SDID results are reported
only as point-estimate robustness checks, without p-values. We therefore use augmented synthetic
control with station fixed effects as the preferred local estimator and use SDID as a robustness check.
This local hierarchy is design-based: augmented synthetic control gives a transparent station-level
counterfactual, and conformal inference can be applied directly to the prediction errors of that
counterfactual.
     Inference for the local augmented synthetic-control estimates is based on conformal methods for
counterfactual and synthetic-control settings (Chernozhukov et al. 2021). The intuition is simple.
Under the null hypothesis of no treatment effect, the post-opening prediction error should not look
unusually large relative to the prediction errors observed before the metro opened. Conformal
inference tests candidate treatment effects by shifting the post-treatment outcomes and asking which
shifts make the full sequence of prediction errors look consistent with the pre-treatment errors. This
is attractive in the local setting because it does not require a large number of donor stations.
     We use a leave-one-out spatial placebo exercise to assess whether the conformal procedure
over-rejects and whether the estimated reduction is specific to Centro. We shift treatment status
across the monitoring network by treating each station in turn as if it were exposed, construct
its synthetic counterfactual from the remaining stations, and apply the same conformal inference


                                                         12
procedure. This exercise is not the main source of statistical inference for Centro. With only eight
stations, any rank-based comparison is coarse and has a natural lower bound of 1/8. Instead, it
checks the design’s internal logic: the method should not find significant reductions at stations where
the destination-access mechanism predicts none, and the reduction should appear only at Centro.
Belisario is the key placebo because it is nearly as close to Line 1 as Centro but outside the historic
center.
     As described in the data section, we estimate effects on three post-opening windows for all
pollutants. The full post-opening window keeps all available weeks after the metro began operating,
including the late-2024 disruption period. We also report a clean pre-disruption window because
Ecuador’s energy crisis generated citywide pollution shocks that could mask or confound metro-
related changes in air quality. This is not a sample-cleaning exclusion, but an estimand choice: it
isolates the period after the metro opened and before the major citywide disruption. Alternative win-
dows show how the estimates change when the disruption period and the more limited post-disruption
coverage are included. PM2.5 is the preferred local outcome because all eight monitoring stations
report it, preserving the largest donor pool. Other pollutants provide robustness and mechanism
checks, but are estimated with a smaller donor pool because San Antonio does not report those
series.
     The satellite analysis estimates a separate, city-level estimand. Instead of comparing monitors
within Quito, it compares Quito as an urban area to a synthetic combination of other Latin American
urban areas. This design asks whether the metro generated a detectable aggregate change in pollution
at the city scale. It is not designed to detect localized changes inside the historic center. In this setting,
the comparison set is much larger. Quito is compared to a donor pool of Latin American urban
areas ranked by similarity, and the primary specification uses synthetic difference-in-differences.
This choice follows the structure of the method: with many candidate donor cities and repeated pre-
treatment observations, SDID can use both unit weights and time weights to construct an aggregate
counterfactual for Quito (Arkhangelsky et al. 2021).
     For SDID and synthetic-control estimates in the satellite analysis, we report placebo standard
errors and Wald-type tests, complemented by rank-based placebo diagnostics. The placebo standard
errors are computed by repeatedly reassigning treatment to donor units and using the dispersion of
those placebo estimates as the sampling variability of the estimator. The Wald tests then compare the
estimated effect to this placebo-based standard error. This approximation is more informative in the
city-level analysis than in the local station analysis because the satellite donor pool is large enough to
generate a meaningful distribution of placebo estimates. We retain rank-based permutation p-values
as a secondary check reported in the appendix; they lead to the same substantive conclusions.
     We also estimate augmented synthetic-control specifications in the satellite analysis, but interpret
them as robustness checks. The same flexibility that makes augmented synthetic control useful


                                                     13
in the local station setting can become less desirable when the donor pool is large and the pre-
treatment satellite panel is high-dimensional. In that setting, ridge regularization helps, but we still
prefer SDID as the primary estimator because it imposes a more transparent weighting structure for
the aggregate city-level comparison. The hierarchy is therefore reversed across the two analyses:
augmented synthetic control is primary locally and robustness at the city level, while synthetic
difference-in-differences is primary at the city level and robustness locally.
    The two designs answer different questions. The satellite analysis asks whether Quito experienced
a detectable citywide pollution change relative to comparable Latin American urban areas. The local
station analysis asks whether PM2.5 changed at the car-constrained destination where the mechanism
predicts the strongest early effect, and whether the same pattern is absent at an almost equidistant
non-historic center monitor. The empirical strategy therefore does not require a smooth distance
gradient from the metro corridor. With only eight monitoring stations, a short post-opening period,
and a major late-2024 disruption, the local design is better suited to testing the sharper prediction
that the response should appear at Centro rather than at Belisario. Evidence on the full spatial extent
of the effect remains an open question.

5     Results
The results support one main claim: Metro Line 1 is associated with a localized reduction in fine-
particulate pollution at Quito’s historic center, where the urban form makes car access difficult and
metro access especially valuable. The evidence is strongest for PM2.5 at Centro. Belisario, which is
almost as close to the metro corridor but outside the historic center, does not show the same pattern.
This contrast points to a localized particulate effect at the predicted destination, rather than a generic
proximity effect along the corridor. The results do not establish the full spatial extent of the effect,
and they do not by themselves prove the traffic channel.
    The empirical prediction is specific. Evidence for a destination-access mechanism should show
a PM2.5 decline at Centro, no comparable decline at Belisario, and no similar pattern across the rest
of the monitoring network. Evidence for a simple corridor-proximity mechanism would instead
predict similar declines at both Centro and Belisario. We organize the results around this distinction.

5.1   Local PM2.5 effects at Centro
Figure 5 shows the weekly event-study estimates for the preferred Centro specification. The pre-
opening gaps fluctuate around zero, while the post-opening estimates move below zero during the
first months of operation and deepen through 2024. During the early post-opening period before
the blackout and wildfire disruptions, the average effect is −0.130 log points. The estimate is
nearly identical, −0.132 log points, when later post-opening weeks are added but the disruption
weeks are excluded. The figure therefore shows that the preferred estimate is not driven by a single


                                                   14
post-opening week or by the late-2024 disruption. The gradual deepening of the effect is consistent
with a response that builds as metro ridership grows rather than a discrete shift at opening.
    Table 5 reports the local PM2.5 estimates. The table is organized around the identification logic.
The first column treats Centro and Belisario jointly as the two monitoring stations closest to Line 1.
This proximity-based specification produces negative estimates in the clean windows, but the effects
are not statistically significant. This suggests that the PM2.5 result is concentrated at the predicted
historic-center destination rather than simply tracing proximity to the metro corridor.
    The second column treats Belisario as the affected station and excludes Centro from the donor
pool. Belisario is almost as close to the metro corridor as Centro, but its estimates are small,
imprecise, and not statistically significant in the clean windows. This first establishes Belisario as
a placebo-treated station with no comparable response, which in turn supports using it as a valid
donor for Centro.
    The third column treats Centro as the affected station and removes Belisario from the donor
pool. This conservative specification produces larger negative point estimates, 17.0 percent in
the pre-disruption window and 14.2 percent in the donut window, but the estimates are imprecise.
The lack of precision is informative: removing Belisario also removes the closest and most useful
comparison station for Centro.
    The preferred specification, in the final column, treats Centro as the affected station and retains
Belisario in the donor pool. This specification estimates a 12.2 percent reduction in PM2.5 in the pre-
disruption window, with a two-sided conformal p-value of 0.011. The estimate is nearly unchanged
in the donut window, at 12.4 percent. The full post-opening window gives a larger reduction of 15.1
percent, but because it includes the late-2024 disruption, we interpret the pre-disruption estimate as
the cleanest local effect.
    The preferred specification delivers the statistically significant Centro effect while also providing
the strongest pre-opening fit. Its pre-treatment RMSPE is 0.129, compared with 0.185 to 0.205 in
the other specifications, and its post-to-pre RMSPE ratio rises to 1.98 in the pre-disruption window.
This means that retaining Belisario improves the counterfactual fit before treatment and reveals a
post-opening divergence that is large relative to the pre-opening prediction error. The next section
broadens the test to the full monitoring network, asking whether the Centro result is unique among
Quito’s monitors and whether other stations show comparable evidence of a post-opening PM2.5
reduction.

5.4   Citywide satellite estimates
The satellite analysis is not designed to identify the historic-center mechanism directly. It asks a
broader question: whether Quito as an urban area experienced a detectable aggregate change in
pollution relative to comparable Latin American urban areas. Table 8 reports the citywide SDID
estimates for AOD, the satellite particulate proxy, across donor pools. The estimates are negative
across all donor pools and windows. In the preferred 100-city donor pool, selected using the
pre-specified Mahalanobis-distance rule, AOD falls by 0.113 log points during the first months of
operation before the blackout and wildfire disruptions. The corresponding two-sided Wald p-value
from placebo standard errors is 0.035.
    The pool-walk supports the same conclusion. In the primary pre-disruption window, the AOD
estimate is negative in every donor pool, ranging from −0.100 to −0.145 log points. The 95 percent
confidence intervals exclude zero in every pool, although the upper bound in the 50-city pool sits
essentially at zero, indicating that precision is weakest in the most restrictive donor pool. The donut
and full-window estimates are also negative and generally larger in magnitude. The result therefore
does not depend on selecting the 100-city pool because it is significant; that pool is the preferred
specification because it follows the pre-specified donor-selection rule.
    The AOD results point in the same particulate direction as the local PM2.5 evidence, but they
should not be interpreted as a clean citywide metro effect. The satellite estimand is different from the
local station estimand. AOD measures aerosol loading in the atmospheric column rather than surface
PM2.5 , and the broader Ecuadorian environment in 2024 may have affected Quito differently from
other Latin American cities. The magnitude of the citywide AOD estimates is also large relative to
what a single metro line would likely produce at the urban scale. For these reasons, we interpret the
satellite analysis as a bounded regional benchmark: it corroborates the direction of the particulate
result, but it is not the main causal test.

6   Conclusion
This paper studies whether the air-quality effects of a new metro line can be concentrated within a
city. Using the opening of Quito Metro Line 1, we compare pollution changes at the historic center
with changes at other monitoring stations and then place the local evidence in a broader citywide
comparison using satellite data. The main result is a localized decline in fine particulate pollution
at Centro, the monitoring station inside Quito’s historic center. PM2.5 falls significantly, by about
12 percent, during the first months of operation before Ecuador’s late-2024 blackout and wildfire
disruptions. The effect is not mirrored at Belisario, a station that is nearly as close to the metro
corridor but outside the historic center.
    The spatial pattern is central to the interpretation. The results do not support a simple proximity
story in which pollution falls mechanically near the rail corridor. Instead, the evidence points to a
destination-based response: the metro appears to matter most where it changes access to a dense

transport intervention changes the relative attractiveness of driving.
    The satellite analysis supports this interpretation only in a limited sense. Aerosol optical depth
moves in the same particulate direction as the local PM2.5 result, while satellite gas outcomes do
not show a consistent pattern. We therefore interpret the citywide evidence as complementary,
not as the paper’s main causal test. Satellite measures capture pollution at a different spatial and
atmospheric scale, and Quito’s broader post-opening period coincided with national shocks that
complicate citywide comparisons. The strongest evidence remains the station-level result at the
location where the mechanism predicts the clearest early effect.
    These findings have two implications for policy. First, large transit investments may not generate
broad environmental benefits on their own. Their local air-quality effects are more likely to appear
where the new system makes car use less attractive relative to public transport. In dense destinations,
especially places with limited parking, constrained street networks, and high pedestrian activity,
rail access may produce cleaner local environments even before broader citywide changes are
visible. Second, transport policy may need to pair supply-side investments, such as metros, with
complementary measures that limit the convenience of driving in the areas where modal shift is
most valuable.
    The paper also points to the limits of the current evidence. The results document where the
early particulate response appears, but they do not directly measure the traffic channel. Future work


                                                          21
should combine air-quality data with ridership, congestion, vehicle counts, parking behavior, and
origin-destination travel patterns to test whether the observed pollution changes reflect reduced
vehicle access to the historic center, changes in bus circulation, lower congestion, or other local
activity responses. As Quito’s metro matures and ridership grows, these data will be important for
understanding whether the early localized gains expand, persist, or depend on additional policies
that reshape travel behavior.
